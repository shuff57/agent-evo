# Voice profile: Steven Huff

Derived from writing samples the author provided (equity/teaching statement paragraphs, reproduced at the bottom of this file). Everything in the profile below is an observation about those samples. Nothing here is invented.

**Published deliberately, by the author, for others to use.** Two things follow
from that:

- **Use it as a register, not as an identity.** It is a plain, unshowy
  professional voice, and it is offered as one worth borrowing. Borrowing how
  someone writes is ordinary; passing work off as theirs is not. Do not use
  this profile to produce writing attributed to Steven Huff, or to sign, submit
  or publish anything in his name.
- **Your own sample beats it every time.** If you have writing of your own,

**This profile outranks the style rules in SKILL.md where they conflict.** See §Voice Calibration: matching the author beats scrubbing the tell.

## Punctuation

- **No em dashes, no en dashes.** Zero across the entire sample. The §14 ban matches this author's habits exactly, so apply it without hesitation.
- **Colons carry the reveal.** "gave me something a credential never could: firsthand understanding of skilled, cognitively demanding work outside an academic setting." Keep this move; it is his, not a tell.
- **Commas do the joining work,** including deliberate comma splices for rhetorical balance: "Diversity highlights what makes us different, inclusion reveals what makes us similar." Do not repair these into semicolons or two sentences.
- **Semicolons are rare to absent** in the sample. Do not introduce them when replacing an em dash. Use a period or a comma.
- **No bold, no bullets, no inline headers** anywhere in his prose. Reserve those for structural document elements (tables, policy labels), never for emphasis inside a paragraph.

## Constructions to preserve, not strip

These appear in SKILL.md's AI-pattern list but are genuine habits of this author. Leave them alone.

- **Negative parallelism (§9).** A signature move, used for real contrast rather than filler:
  - "not just access to instruction, but access to comprehensible materials"
  - "they struggle not because they cannot do the mathematics, but because the materials were never built with them in mind"
  - "Supporting older systems by simply providing resources does not make college truly accessible."
  Keep it where the two halves carry different content. Strip it only when the negative half is empty ("not a separate assignment").
- **Rule of three (§10), in closing sentences only.** "expertise takes many forms, rigor lives in many places, and the most important thing an educator can do is remain genuinely open to being taught." He uses triads to land a paragraph, not to pad a list. Keep closer triads; still cut triads used to fake comprehensiveness mid-paragraph.
- **Value vocabulary (§4).** He genuinely writes *strive*, *commitment*, *dedication*, *equitable*, *rigorous*, *genuinely*, *mindful*. These are his words in earnest, not promotional inflation. Do not flatten them.

## Sentence and paragraph shape

- **Long sentences, roughly 25 to 40 words,** with multiple subordinate clauses. He does not write staccato. Do not break his long sentences into short punchy ones, and do not manufacture short-sentence drama (§31).
- **First person, direct.** "I developed," "I undertook," "I can say it with genuine conviction." No hedging stacks, no passive evasion.
- **Paragraph arc:** claim, then personal experience as evidence, then what it taught him, then the implication for practice. He does not open with a thesis-restating warm-up line.
- **Concrete proper nouns and numbers, always.** San Joaquin County. Norfield Industries. More than 90 hours. Summer 2025. bookSHelf. OGRE. He hoards specifics rather than rounding them off. Never replace one of his specifics with a generality, and never invent a new one to imitate the habit.
- **Acronyms are expanded on first use** with the expansion first: "Ollama Grading Rubric Evaluator (OGRE)."
- **Endings are reflective claims, not summaries.** He closes on a position he is willing to defend. Do not append a generic upbeat send-off (§25), and do not delete his reflective closer as though it were one.

## Register

Formal but personal. He writes about institutions in plain language and about himself without self-deprecation or salesmanship. When the document being edited is a syllabus, policy, or other reference text, hold the register formal and drop the first-person reflection; the profile above governs rhythm and punctuation, not the injection of voice into documents that should be neutral.

## Source samples

> Being from an ethnically and culturally diverse community like San Joaquin County, I have many friends and family who struggled with entering and navigating the higher education system. Once in college, I noticed classmates struggling with meeting deadlines, finding resources, and understanding the unwritten rules of academic life. Realizing I was fortunate to have a family providing guidance and institutional knowledge, I found myself supporting those around me through their higher education journey. With years dedicated to developing accessible and inclusive practices, I strive to make students from diverse backgrounds equitable members of the learning community.

> To fully embrace diversity, a college must actively strive toward inclusivity and an equitable experience for students from all backgrounds. Diversity highlights what makes us different, inclusion reveals what makes us similar. We should strive for a higher education environment that reflects our students and the worlds they are a part of. Supporting older systems by simply providing resources does not make college truly accessible. Sometimes we need to tear down and rebuild with all students in mind. With a continued commitment to serving historically underrepresented populations I want to participate in and strengthen the systems that provide an uninhibited learning experience within a diverse place of higher education.

> My dedication and commitment to supporting diverse populations drives how I approach instructional design. One persistent equity gap in mathematics and statistics education is not just access to instruction, but access to comprehensible materials. In particular, many students are first-generation, English language learners, or working full-time and they struggle not because they cannot do the mathematics, but because the materials were never built with them in mind. To address this directly, I developed bookSHelf, an AI-powered pipeline that transforms raw textbook content into a Student Enhanced Edition, rebuilding the textbook experience from the ground up with those students in mind.

> I am also mindful that many of my students do not arrive at college through a conventional academic path. For example some come from trades, manufacturing, and technical backgrounds, and carry expertise that the traditional classroom rarely acknowledges or builds upon. Having developed an Embedded Systems and Robotics course, I undertook more than 90 hours of rigorous industry-based technical training at Norfield Industries in summer 2025 to ensure my instruction would reflect current professional practice. That experience working alongside machinists to fabricate precision components gave me something a credential never could: firsthand understanding of skilled, cognitively demanding work outside an academic setting. When I tell a student from a trade or working-class background that mathematics is relevant to their world, I can say it with genuine conviction because I have stood on that production floor myself.

> Reflection has taught me that building equitable tools is only half the work. The harder half is examining the assumptions baked into them and into myself. The mental precision required on the Norfield production floor was as rigorous as anything in a university setting, just expressed in a different language and valued by a different culture. That reflection reminded me how often students from trade and working-class backgrounds are implicitly treated as less prepared for abstract thinking, when in fact they have simply been prepared differently. It has made me more careful and more humble as an instructor.

> Equity reflection also shapes how I approach grading. Feedback quality matters as much as the grade itself, yet giving every student substantive, individualized feedback is among the most time-consuming aspects of teaching. I developed Ollama Grading Rubric Evaluator (OGRE), an AI-powered rubric evaluating assistant, specifically to maintain consistency across all students with the same rubric, the same depth of feedback, and the goal of minimizing implicit bias. Consistency in grading is itself an equity issue, as evaluator fatigue and unconscious bias tend to drift in over long grading sessions. Using OGRE to carefully structure the evaluation process helps to ensure fair and equitable feedback.

> My experience growing up in San Joaquin County and my time on the production floor at Norfield both remind me that expertise takes many forms, rigor lives in many places, and the most important thing an educator can do is remain genuinely open to being taught.
